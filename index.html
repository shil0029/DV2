A STRONG NORTHERN CONNECTION

35.1%

More than a third of adults in the Northern Territory.

That was the highest participation estimate in the 2020 national survey. Western Australia followed at 24.4%; Victoria recorded 19.0%.

These are shares of residents, not a ranking of how many people fish. A smaller population can have a stronger fishing habit.

Survey estimates carry uncertainty. The report did not find clear evidence of a state-level change between 2018 and 2020 once survey uncertainty was taken into account.

READ THE SCOPE

The national picture tells us who fishes. For a detailed view of catches, we turn to NSW’s survey of households holding long-term recreational fishing licences. These estimates describe that population fishing in NSW waters.

20 MONITORED SPECIES & GROUPS

Bream lead this catch.

The 2023–24 estimates put bream at about 1.27 million individual catches, followed by dusky flathead at 893,000.

The rectangles compare the catch counts for 20 species and groups tracked across surveys. They include finfish and two groups of crustaceans.

This selection is not the whole NSW catch. A larger catch can reflect fishing activity, fish availability, or other factors; it is not a direct measure of a fish population.

READ ACROSS, THEN DOWN

One fishing hobby.
Very different catches.

Follow a row to see how a species’ release share changes. Scan down a column to compare species in the same year.

THE RIPPLE

A day on the water.
A wider economic footprint.

Fishing supports activity beyond the water: buying tackle, travelling, maintaining boats and staying overnight. The national survey modelled both the direct contribution and the flow-on effects through suppliers and other businesses in 2018–19.

FOLLOW THE SELECTED ORIGIN

Victoria’s spending reaches beyond Victoria.

A fishing purchase can support a job in the place where it is made and further jobs through the supply chain. That is why a benefit can appear in a different state to the fisher’s home.

FTE combines part-time and full-time work into full-time equivalents. These are modelled contributions, not counts of people hired by fishing businesses.

MORE THAN A ROD AND REEL

Gear, travel and boats all contribute.

Fishing gear and tackle generated the largest modelled GDP contribution among these categories. Overnight travel followed.

Gross domestic product (GDP) measures the value added to the economy. It is different from the amount anglers paid at the checkout.

BACK TO THE WATER

A shared hobby.
Many ways to take part.

From the Northern Territory’s high participation to NSW’s diverse catches, fishing takes different forms across Australia. Its story includes the people who fish, the organisms they catch and release, and the communities their spending supports.

SOURCES & METHODS

Read the data.

Two research sources, different populations. Each chart keeps its own date, unit and denominator.

  1. National Social and Economic Survey of Recreational Fishers 2018–2021

    Moore et al. (2023), FRDC, ABARES and University of Canberra. National and state adult participation use the 2020 survey (n = 8,736). The fisher counts use 2018 participation and the report’s population estimates. Economic modelling covers 2018–19.

    CC BY 3.0 Australia. The report discusses survey error and method differences. Values were transcribed from tables or explicit numeric labels, never estimated from chart lengths. Interstate job flows aggregate the report’s capital-city and regional matrix to states and territories.

  2. Survey of recreational fishing in NSW, 2023/24

    NSW Department of Primary Industries and Regional Development. Appendix 3 supplies catch, kept and released estimates for 20 monitored species or groups in 2017/18, 2019/20, 2021/22 and 2023/24. The survey population is households with long-term NSW recreational fishing licences, fishing in NSW waters.
